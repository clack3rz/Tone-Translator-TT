SECTION 1 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 PURPOSE & MANDATE
Tone Translator (TT) is developing a platform-independent AI Sound Engineer
architecture dedicated to analyzing, recreating, designing, comparing, and
refining recorded electric guitar tones. Following the infrastructure established
in Phase 1A (isolation, dual-stream execution), Phase 1B (checkpoint contracts,
layer contracts, run tracing, and observability), and Phase 1C.2 (the provenance
and knowledge audit of Current TT), Phase 1C.3 defines the professional standard
and target maturity against which all future sound engineering capabilities must
be measured.

This specification—Phase 1C.3a—independently defines the professional standard of
competence before any comparison with Current TT is made. Under the Critical
Independence Rule, this standard is not calibrated against what Current TT
currently supports, how its prompts are written, which static recipes it uses,
or the technical limitations of AmpliTube 5. It establishes what a world-class,
highly discerning professional sound engineer must know, interpret, diagnose,
decide, and verify when working with recorded electric guitar signals.

1.2 METHODOLOGY & THE FIVE-DIMENSIONAL ASSESSMENT MODEL
Professional competence cannot be reduced to a flat checklist of facts or a list
of gear models. To evaluate genuine capability, this standard defines competence
across five distinct, interdependent dimensions for every engineering domain:
  1. Knowledge: Understanding underlying physical, acoustic, electrical, and
     psychoacoustic mechanisms, causal principles, and whole-system interactions.
  2. Evidence Interpretation: The ability to separate objective acoustic measurements
     and observations from inferences, assumptions, and contextual background.
  3. Diagnosis: The discipline of tracing perceived acoustic symptoms back through
     competing causal hypotheses before selecting an intervention.
  4. Decision-Making: The capacity to select defensible interventions while
     explicitly balancing objectives, physical constraints, trade-offs, and
     downstream dependencies.
  5. Outcome Reasoning: The ability to predict specific acoustic consequences,
     define measurable verification criteria, evaluate results, and iteratively
     revise hypotheses when interventions yield unexpected outcomes.

1.3 PLATFORM-INDEPENDENT SCOPE
A true Sound Engineer reasons about sound, electricity, vibration, and perception.
The Sound Engineer is solely responsible for determining WHAT acoustic and
electrical transformations must occur and WHY they are necessary. It is never
responsible for HOW a specific modeling platform (e.g., AmpliTube, Helix, Quad
Cortex, Kemper, analog hardware) serializes or executes that intent.

1.4 DOMAIN VS. CROSS-DOMAIN COMPETENCE
Electric guitar tone is non-linear, dynamic, and electro-acoustically coupled.
A change at the guitar volume pot changes the frequency spectrum entering the
first gain stage, which alters the dynamic intermodulation distortion produced
by the vacuum tubes, which shifts the harmonic content exciting the loudspeaker's
mechanical resonance modes, which changes the acoustic wave arriving off-axis at
the microphone diaphragm. Therefore, mastery of isolated domains is insufficient;
professional competence requires systemic cross-domain reasoning across the
entire signal chain.

SECTION 2 — SOUND ENGINEER MISSION & RESPONSIBILITY BOUNDARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 THE SOUND ENGINEER MISSION
The AI Sound Engineer must be capable of:
"Analysing available evidence, forming defensible hypotheses about how a guitar
sound was produced and why it behaves as it does, designing a platform-independent
signal-processing strategy to reproduce or intentionally modify that sound, and
explaining its decisions, uncertainty, constraints, and trade-offs using
professional audio-engineering principles."

2.2 ARCHITECTURAL RESPONSIBILITY BOUNDARIES
The architecture strictly enforces separation of concerns across five stages:

  [1. AI SOUND ENGINEER]
      Responsibility: WHAT + WHY
      - Analyzes acoustic evidence, references, and musical context.
      - Diagnoses root causes of sonic characteristics or defects.
      - Decides signal topology, gain staging, nonlinear behavior, spectral
        interventions, dynamic control, loudspeaker voicing, capture geometry,
        and spatial placement.
      - Emits a platform-independent Semantic Tone Design accompanied by an
        Engineering Decision Record.
      - Evaluates trade-offs, states confidence, and identifies missing evidence.

  [2. SEMANTIC TONE DESIGN (CONTRACT)]
      Responsibility: CANONICAL SPECIFICATION
      - The formal, platform-independent representation of the engineer's intent.
      - Expresses amplifier behavior (preamp topology, tone stack type, sag,
        damping, feedback), acoustic capture (transducer type, polar pattern,
        distance, angle, cone position), and processing blocks in physical and
        perceptual units (dB, Hz, ms, ratio, degrees, centimeters).

  [3. DETERMINISTIC VALIDATION]
      Responsibility: INTEGRITY & SAFETY
      - Verifies that the Semantic Tone Design is physically and logically legal.
      - Checks mathematical bounds, schema conformance, parameter sanity, and
        signal flow consistency.
      - Contains zero creative or aesthetic judgment; purely objective verification.

  [4. PLATFORM TRANSLATOR]
      Responsibility: HOW (PLATFORM MAPPING)
      - Consumes the validated Semantic Tone Design and translates it into the
        gear roster, routing architecture, and parameter topologies of a specific
        target platform (e.g., AmpliTube 5, Kemper, Neural DSP).
      - Handles gear substitution, slot constraints, module order limitations,
        and parameter scaling.

  [5. EXPORTER / SERIALIZER]
      Responsibility: ENCODING & WRITE
      - Converts the platform-specific plan into native machine files (.at5p,
        XML, SysEx, JSON presets).
      - Manages platform GUIDs, checksums, binary headers, and file system writes.

SECTION 3 — MATURITY MODEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The maturity of the Sound Engineer across each domain is evaluated on a six-level
scale (L0 to L5). This model emphasizes diagnostic and reasoning depth rather
than encyclopedic memory.

L0 — ABSENT
  - No meaningful knowledge or capability. Concepts are unrecognized, and
    behavior in this domain is completely random, unrepresented, or missing.

L1 — VOCABULARY
  - Recognizes domain terminology, gear names, and basic concepts, but cannot
    reliably apply them to engineering problems. Relies on superficial keyword
    associations without understanding underlying mechanics.

L2 — CONCEPTUAL
  - Understands relevant physical, electrical, or acoustic principles. Can explain
    why a phenomenon occurs theoretically (e.g., explains proximity effect or
    pre-gain filtering), but lacks the practical reasoning to diagnose complex
    cases or design appropriate interventions independently.

L3 — APPLIED
  - Applies principles correctly to standard, well-defined engineering problems.
    Can design functional solutions and select reasonable interventions when
    evidence is unambiguous and symptoms point directly to a known cause.

L4 — DIAGNOSTIC
  - Handles ambiguous, multi-factor, or contradictory engineering challenges.
    Can separate symptoms from root causes, generate and rank competing causal
    hypotheses from incomplete evidence, compare multiple intervention pathways,
    and explicitly account for immediate engineering trade-offs.

L5 — PROFESSIONAL
  - Demonstrates master-level professional judgment under realistic uncertainty.
    Integrates the domain seamlessly with all other engineering disciplines across
    the whole signal chain. Reasons from incomplete, noisy, or conflicting evidence;
    evaluates multiple defensible solutions; accounts for physical constraints
    and subtle trade-offs; predicts downstream non-linear interactions; avoids
    false precision; clearly states epistemic uncertainty; and systematically
    revises hypotheses when outcome evaluation contradicts predictions.
  - NOTE: L5 does NOT denote omniscience or encyclopedic memorization of every
    commercial product ever manufactured. It denotes the rigorous, disciplined,
    first-principles causal reasoning of an elite recording engineer.

================================================================================
DOMAIN 1: SOURCE & PERFORMANCE
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
The electric guitar signal begins at the instrument. Pickup inductance, resonant
peak, output level, pick material, attack angle, string age, and palm-muting
technique define the frequency-dependent dynamic voltage feeding the first gain
stage. An engineer who cannot decouple source mechanics from amplifier behavior
will misdiagnose downstream issues.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
In guitar tone matching, over 50% of tone discrepancies (e.g., lack of bite,
excessive flub, sluggish transients) originate at the instrument or player's
hands. Treating these purely with amplifier EQ ruins dynamic feel.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands electromagnetic pickup physics (single-coil vs. PAF humbucker vs.
active high-output; coil inductance, resistance, inter-winding capacitance,
resonant frequency peak); magnetic aperture effects (neck vs. bridge placement);
potentiometer and cable capacitive loading (500k vs. 250k pots, cable capacitance
shifting the resonant peak downward); scale length and string tension effects on
inharmonicity and transient snap; pick gauge/material/angle impact on high-frequency
transient chirp; palm muting mechanics (acoustic damping of fundamental vs.
forcing higher harmonic dominance); and input voltage swing (0.1V RMS to 2.5V peak).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Accurately categorizes observed audio cues: distinguishes between high-gain
compression and light playing dynamics; differentiates between pickup resonant
peak emphasis (2.5 kHz - 4.5 kHz) and amplifier presence boost; detects palm-muting
decay characteristics vs. artificial noise gating; and recognizes when input
level is overdriving an input stage prematurely.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When presented with a dark, tubby tone, evaluates whether the source is a neck
pickup, rolled-off guitar tone pot, heavy cable loading, or soft thumb picking
before blaming the amplifier's tone stack or microphone placement.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Designs input-stage conditioning (input impedance matching, input pad/trim, or
pre-filtering) specifically to compensate for source discrepancies without
altering the amplifier's core gain structure.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how changing pickup type or playing style alters the saturation point of
cascaded tube stages; verifies that an input-level adjustment restores touch
sensitivity without thinning out single notes.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Gain Staging (Domain 2): Input voltage dictates first-stage clipping threshold.
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Frequency balance of pickup drives intermodulation.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Pickup resonant peak interacts with preamp EQ.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Assuming all tone differences are caused by amplifier or cabinet settings.
  - Attempting to turn a single-coil into an active humbucker solely using graphic EQ.
  - Ignoring player articulation and assuming palm mutes require a noise gate.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Diagnosing source characteristics, specifying input trim, impedance,
    and pickup compensation requirements.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Enforcing numeric voltage range limits (-1.0 to +1.0).
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Selecting specific proprietary guitar-modeling blocks.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Explains that humbuckers have more midrange and output than single-coils.
  - L3: Recommends rolling off bass when a high-output humbucker creates mud.
  - L4: Distinguishes between pickup-induced low-end muddiness and cabinet proximity
        effect by analyzing transient attack and harmonic decay.
  - L5: Formulates a unified hypothesis integrating player pick angle, pickup
        resonant peak, and input cable capacitance to explain why palm mutes
        flub out while open chords remain brittle, selecting minimal input-stage
        filtering to correct both simultaneously.

DOMAIN 2: SIGNAL LEVEL, GAIN STAGING & HEADROOM
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Guitar amplification is a cascade of non-linear stages. The operating level at each
junction dictates dynamic response, harmonic generation, signal-to-noise ratio,
and touch sensitivity.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Tone quality in high-gain and edge-of-breakup rigs is determined by where gain
occurs and how headroom is managed across stages, not by total gross gain.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands nominal operating levels (-10 dBV vs. +4 dBu vs. instrument level);
dynamic headroom; stage-to-stage transfer curves; grid conduction and blocking
distortion; noise floor accumulation across serial gain blocks; clean boost vs.
clipping boost mechanics; master volume vs. channel volume gain staging; and the
psychoacoustic trap of loudness bias (louder sounds perceived as having more bass
and treble).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Separates true harmonic saturation from perceived loudness; identifies stage
compression by measuring crest factor (peak-to-RMS ratio); detects inter-stage
clipping vs. power-stage saturation; and identifies when high noise floor is
caused by poor gain distribution rather than excessive gain.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Traces muddy or buzzy compression back to its specific originating stage (e.g.,
overdriven FX loop input vs. overdriven phase inverter vs. pinned preamp tube).

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Distributes gain across the signal chain incrementally; balances pedal boost,
preamp gain, loop levels, and power amp drive to achieve the target distortion
envelope while preserving maximum dynamic articulation and minimal idle noise.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts the exact headroom reduction and noise penalty of boosting an upstream
stage; provides level compensation across processing blocks so that A/B comparisons
are strictly loudness-matched.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Source & Performance (Domain 1): Input sensitivity must match guitar output.
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Gain level directly dictates clipping depth.
  - Noise Engineering (Domain 6): Excessive early gain amplifies front-end hiss.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Cranking a single gain knob to 10 while leaving other stages choked at 1.
  - Using digital boost to achieve distortion without accounting for inter-stage headroom.
  - Failing to level-match before evaluating whether a tone modification was an improvement.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying inter-stage gain structure, drive distribution, and level balancing.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Digital audio buffer clipping prevention and float limits.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform-specific dB scaling quirks and knob taper curves.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that gain adds distortion and volume, and too much causes noise.
  - L3: Sets pedal output high and amp gain moderate to tighten a metal rhythm tone.
  - L4: Analyzes crest factor across a 4-stage chain to pinpoint that blocking
        distortion in the second tube stage is causing transient smear.
  - L5: Calculates and balances stage-by-stage headroom across an overdrive pedal,
        tube preamp, effects loop, and power amp, demonstrating that a 3 dB reduction
        in preamp drive combined with a 4 dB increase in power amp master delivers
        identical sustain with vastly superior pick articulation and 6 dB lower noise.

DOMAIN 3: NONLINEAR PROCESSING & DISTORTION
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Distortion is not merely a quantitative parameter (more/less); it is a complex
qualitative spectrum of harmonic generation, dynamic compression, and intermodulation.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
The core character of electric guitar tone—from blues crunch to modern djent—is
defined by the harmonic structure, symmetry, and envelope of its distortion.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands hard diode clipping vs. soft tube saturation vs. asymmetrical FET
clipping vs. crossover distortion; even-order (2nd, 4th) vs. odd-order (3rd, 5th)
harmonic spectra; dynamic bias shift and duty-cycle modulation under heavy signal
drive; intermodulation distortion (IMD) resulting from multi-note chord voicings;
frequency-dependent saturation (saturating low frequencies vs. saturating highs);
and the subjective textures of distortion (granular, fuzzy, smooth, articulate,
saturated, tight, loose, raw, polished).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies the primary distortion character from recorded audio: analyzes harmonic
density, identifies whether clipping is symmetrical (odd harmonics, square wave,
hollow buzz) or asymmetrical (even harmonics, warm, octaver-like undertones);
measures dynamic collapse during chords; and detects unpleasant IMD hash.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a chord sounds harsh and dissonant, determines whether the cause is excessive
odd-order clipping in a solid-state boost, severe IMD in a high-gain preamp, or
speaker cone cry.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Selects and configures nonlinear architectures to meet aesthetic goals: chooses
soft-clipping asymmetrical stages for open, dynamic blues lead; specifies cascading
symmetrical stages preceded by low-end roll-off for fast, articulate thrash rhythm.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how an increase in drive will compress transients and expand sustain;
anticipates how chord voicings will translate through the designed nonlinear stages.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Upstream spectrum dictates which harmonics generate.
  - Dynamics & Transients (Domain 5): Nonlinear saturation inherently acts as a fast limiter.
  - Amplifier Behaviour (Domain 8): Preamp tubes, phase inverters, and power tubes
    contribute distinct non-linear behaviors.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Treating distortion as a single 0-100 scalar slider.
  - Believing all clipping must come from the amplifier's preamp circuit.
  - Attempting to cure fizzy distortion by cutting treble downstream instead of
    addressing harsh non-linear clipping upstream.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying clipping curves, harmonic balance, stage cascading, and
    non-linear compression behavior.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Math functions for wave-shaping algorithms (tanh, arctan).
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Specific model names of overdrive pedals or amp channels.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Explains that tube distortion sounds warmer because of even harmonics.
  - L3: Selects an overdrive pedal with a mid-hump to create smooth lead distortion.
  - L4: Diagnoses that harshness in a rhythm track is caused by symmetrical hard
        clipping generating excessive 7th and 9th harmonics, recommending an
        asymmetrical clipping stage with softer knee.
  - L5: Designs a multi-stage non-linear cascade combining a mild Germanium diode
        soft-clipper, a high-headroom tube preamp, and a pushed power section,
        explaining how dynamic bias shift maintains chord articulation during full
        barre chords while providing rich, singing 2nd-harmonic sustain on single notes.

DOMAIN 4: FREQUENCY & SPECTRAL ENGINEERING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
EQ is the most powerful tool in audio engineering, but its efficacy depends
entirely on WHERE it is applied relative to non-linear stages.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
A 3 dB boost at 800 Hz before an amplifier produces an entirely different acoustic
result than the same boost after the amplifier or at the microphone. A professional
must know the causal location for every spectral intervention.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands filter topologies (high-pass, low-pass, resonant, shelf, parametric
bell, graphic); Q-factor and bandwidth; phase shift across minimum-phase filters;
pre-distortion spectral shaping (filtering before clipping alters harmonic generation);
tone stack architectures (Fender/Marshall/Vox passive FMV vs. active James/Baxandall);
post-distortion EQ (spectral balancing of generated harmonics); loudspeaker transfer
curves; capture filtering; and masking theory in multi-instrument mixes.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Accurately reads spectral balance from audio evidence: distinguishes between
fundamental energy (80 Hz - 250 Hz), body/wood (250 Hz - 500 Hz), nasal/boxiness
(600 Hz - 1 kHz), forward bite/vocal cut (1.5 kHz - 3.5 kHz), harshness/fizz
(4 kHz - 7 kHz), and air/fret sizzle (8 kHz - 12 kHz); identifies whether a
spectral deficit is caused by source, filtering, or phase cancellation.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a guitar tone is described as "muddy," determines whether the mud is caused by:
  (a) excessive sub-200 Hz energy driving the preamp into flabby distortion,
  (b) a boxy 400 Hz accumulation in the cabinet enclosure,
  (c) microphone proximity effect, or
  (d) downstream mix masking from the bass guitar.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Selects the exact causal location for spectral intervention:
  - Shapes pre-distortion spectrum to control distortion texture and tightness.
  - Adjusts amplifier tone stack for primary musical character.
  - Uses post-distortion/rack EQ for surgical resonance removal and mix pocketing.
  - Uses microphone selection/placement for organic acoustic high/low balance.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts that cutting low frequencies pre-distortion will clean up note articulation
without leaving the final tone thin (since post-distortion low-end can be restored);
verifies that an EQ curve does not introduce unmusical phase smearing.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Spectrum entering distortion dictates harmonic output.
  - Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Mechanical speaker rolloff is a massive natural filter.
  - Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Mic angle and distance act as primary acoustic filters.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Placing an EQ at the end of the chain to fix an upstream distortion problem.
  - Boosting high frequencies on a fizzy amp to get "clarity."
  - Applying extreme, narrow-band surgical EQ notches when physical microphone
    repositioning would achieve a smoother result.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Deciding spectral curves, filter types, and exact causal placement in the chain.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Digital biquad filter coefficient calculations.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Restricting EQ designs to arbitrary 2-band or 10-band platform limits.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that boosting mids helps a guitar cut through a mix.
  - L3: Cuts bass before distortion to tighten the low end for metal rhythm.
  - L4: Separates a 4 kHz fizz problem into two distinct causes: excessive diode
        clipping harmonics (cured pre-gain) and an on-axis condenser resonance
        (cured by moving the mic 15 degrees off-axis).
  - L5: Engineers a complete four-point spectral strategy: a pre-distortion 18 dB/oct
        high-pass filter at 120 Hz to eliminate intermodulation flub; a 750 Hz mid-emphasis
        to drive core crunch; a post-distortion graphic scoop at 400 Hz to remove
        cabinet boxiness; and an off-axis dynamic microphone placement to naturally
        steep-roll frequencies above 6 kHz without electronic filtering.

DOMAIN 5: DYNAMICS, ENVELOPE & TRANSIENTS
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Electric guitar articulation relies on the temporal envelope: the initial pick
transient (0-20 ms), the settling bloom, sustain, and decay.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Playing feel, punch, and rhythm pocket depend on how transients are preserved
or modified. Mismanaged dynamics turn aggressive playing into a flat, lifeless buzz.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands transient response; compressor attack, release, threshold, ratio, and
knee mechanics; peak limiting vs. RMS compression; optical vs. VCA vs. FET vs.
variable-mu compression characteristics; amplifier power supply sag (rectifier tube
voltage drop under load creating dynamic envelope bloom and compression); dynamic
range preservation; and transient-to-body ratios.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Evaluates transient shape from audio: detects whether pick attack is sharp, rounded,
squashed, or smeared; identifies whether sustain is natural acoustic resonance or
heavy non-linear limiting; measures dynamic range to determine if playing feel
remains intact.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Diagnoses whether a lack of punch is caused by an over-compressed front-end pedal,
excessive preamp gain, a heavily sagging power supply, or a slow speaker transient
response.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Selects dynamic interventions based on musical role: uses fast, transparent limiting
for percussive funk strumming; configures a slow-attack compressor to accentuate pick
transients before medium-gain crunch; relies on natural power amp sag for organic
touch-sensitive blues bloom.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how dynamic processing will affect the signal-to-noise ratio and sustained
decay; verifies that compression does not exaggerate finger squeaks or pick chirp.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Source & Performance (Domain 1): Pick attack and player touch drive initial transients.
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Saturation is inherently dynamic compression.
  - Amplifier Behaviour (Domain 8): Power amp sag provides dynamic envelope shaping.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Using a compressor as a default "make it sound professional" insert.
  - Cranking preamp gain to get sustain, completely obliterating pick articulation.
  - Setting compressor attack too fast, choking the initial life out of notes.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying dynamic goals, envelope timing (attack/release), and compressor roles.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Sidechain buffer mechanics and peak-detection DSP.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Commercial pedal names (e.g., Dyna Comp, Ross).

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that compressors make quiet sounds louder and loud sounds quieter.
  - L3: Places a compressor pedal before a clean amp to increase sustain.
  - L4: Distinguishes between tube rectifier sag (dynamic envelope bloom) and optical
        pedal compression, selecting power amp sag to preserve touch-responsiveness.
  - L5: Engineers a dynamic response where a subtle VCA compressor with a 30 ms attack
        lets pick attack slice through cleanly before clamping down 2 dB, perfectly
        timing the release to 120 ms to match the song's quarter-note tempo and
        create a rhythmic breathing sustain.

DOMAIN 6: NOISE ENGINEERING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
High-gain electric guitar rigs are inherently high-noise environments. Professional
sound engineering manages noise at its source rather than destroying tone with
heavy, crude gating.
TARGET MATURITY: L4 (Supporting)
WHY THIS TARGET:
While critical for high-gain production, noise control is a supporting discipline
to core tone design. L4 provides complete diagnostic capability to trace noise
origins, optimize signal-to-noise ratios, and tune gates without compromising sustain.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands 50/60 Hz mains hum and ground loops; RF interference and single-coil
buzz; Johnson-Nyquist thermal resistance noise (hiss) amplified through cascading
gain stages; power supply ripple; noise gate mechanics (open/close thresholds,
attack, hold, release, attenuation depth, frequency-conscious sidechain filtering);
and the acoustic difference between idle noise and in-performance signal-to-noise ratio.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies noise types from audio recordings: distinguishes between single-coil 60 Hz
hum, electromagnetic buzzy hash, thermal preamp hiss, digital clock bleed, and
pickup microphonics; identifies gate chattering, premature note cutoff, and unnatural
decay clamping.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Investigates the root cause of noise before applying suppression: determines whether
noise is entering at the guitar, an unshielded overdrive pedal, high preamp gain,
or a noisy effects loop buffer.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Selects noise mitigation strategies hierarchically:
  1. Minimize noise generation via proper gain staging.
  2. Filter sub-audible low rumble and ultrasonic hash.
  3. Apply frequency-selective sidechain gating.
  4. Use transparent downward expansion rather than hard gating when natural note
     decay must be preserved.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Sets gate threshold and release times to match musical tempo and playing style;
verifies that quiet sustained notes and palm-muted staccato rhythms are both handled
cleanly without audible pumping or cutoff.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Source & Performance (Domain 1): Single-coil vs. humbucker pickup shielding.
  - Gain Staging (Domain 2): Front-end noise is multiplied exponentially by downstream gain.
  - Dynamics & Transients (Domain 5): Gate envelopes directly impact note decay.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Clamping a hard noise gate across every high-gain preset by default.
  - Setting gate threshold so high that natural note decay chops off with an audible fizz.
  - Treating 60 Hz ground hum by cranking a noise gate instead of fixing ground isolation.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Diagnosing noise origins, specifying gate parameters, and choosing
    expansion vs. gating.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Audio silence detection and gate DSP buffers.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform gate pedal simulations.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that high-gain amps hiss and need a noise gate.
  - L3: Sets a noise gate threshold to silence the amp when the guitarist stops playing.
  - L4: Analyzes audio to discover that 120 Hz power supply ripple is intermodulating
        with an overdrive pedal, inserting a steep pre-distortion notch at 120 Hz
        which eliminates the hash and allows the noise gate threshold to be lowered
        by 10 dB, restoring natural decay.

DOMAIN 7: SIGNAL TOPOLOGY & PROCESSING ORDER
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
The order and routing of processing elements in an audio path dictate their
interrelationships. Non-linear blocks make processing order irreversible.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Professional guitar tone design relies on sophisticated topological architectures
(pre-amp vs. post-amp, series vs. parallel, wet/dry/wet, dual-cab blending). An
engineer restricted to a fixed pedal-board order cannot solve modern production problems.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands non-commutative processing (A followed by B != B followed by A when
non-linear stages are involved); pre-gain stompbox routing; amplifier effects loop
insertion (post-preamp, pre-power amp); post-power amp and post-cabinet studio rack
routing; parallel processing topologies (clean blend, parallel distortion, frequency-split
processing); wet/dry and wet/dry/wet routing; stereo imaging and dual-amplifier
phase-coherent architectures.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Deduces the signal topology of a recorded reference: identifies whether delay repeats
are clean and distinct (post-distortion effects loop) or dirty and compressed (pre-amp
delay); recognizes parallel clean-blend bass/guitar rigs; and identifies stereo
panning vs. mono-collapsed processing.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a time-based effect (delay/reverb/chorus) sounds muddy, washed out, or
distorted, diagnoses whether the problem is improper serial placement before a
high-gain stage rather than the effect's internal parameters.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Designs custom routing architectures driven by engineering intent:
  - Places filters/wah/boost pre-distortion for harmonic shaping.
  - Places modulation/delay in the post-distortion loop or post-cab rack to preserve
    clarity and spatial depth.
  - Routes dual cabinets in phase-aligned parallel paths to combine contrasting
    acoustic voicings.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how altering the position of an EQ or compressor relative to the amplifier
will completely change the dynamic feel and distortion texture; validates that
parallel routing paths sum without comb filtering.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Nonlinear stages make order matter fundamentally.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Pre-EQ vs. post-EQ behavior.
  - Phase, Polarity & Multi-Source (Domain 11): Parallel paths require rigorous phase management.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Memorizing a single "correct pedal order" and applying it dogmatically to every tone.
  - Placing delay and reverb before a high-gain preamp unless an explicit lo-fi/shoegaze
    effect is intended.
  - Running parallel paths without verifying phase alignment at the summing junction.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Architectural routing design, serial vs. parallel decisions, and stage placement.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Graph execution ordering and buffer routing memory.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Specific hardware slot limitations of individual modelers.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that delay usually goes after distortion.
  - L3: Places overdrive before the amp and delay in the effects loop.
  - L4: Identifies that an indistinct, cluttered lead tone is caused by running a
        chorus pedal into high-gain preamp distortion, moving the chorus to a
        parallel post-cab studio rack path to restore core punch while adding width.
  - L5: Architects a dual-path hybrid topology: an input split routing low frequencies
        (<250 Hz) through a high-headroom clean path with heavy VCA compression for
        percussive bass weight, and high frequencies (>250 Hz) through an OverScream
        and high-gain British tube stage, summing them coherently before a shared 4x12
        cabinet to achieve a crushing modern rhythm tone with zero mud and zero transient loss.

DOMAIN 8: AMPLIFIER BEHAVIOUR
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
The amplifier is the central electro-acoustic engine of electric guitar tone.
Specifying an amplifier professionally requires defining its physical and acoustic
behavior rather than relying on commercial brand names or cosmetic skins.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
A true Sound Engineer can design the required amplifier behavior (preamp gain
topology, tone stack placement, power amp negative feedback, sag, damping) from
first principles, regardless of whether a specific historical amplifier model is available.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands tube preamp stages (12AX7/ECC83 triode gain stages, cathode bypass
capacitors, load lines, inter-stage attenuation); tone stack designs (passive FMV
with mid-dip, active James/Baxandall, tweed single-knob, Vox cut control) and their
location in the circuit (early vs. late preamp); phase inverter topologies (long-tailed
pair vs. cathodyne) and their overdrive characteristics; power amp classes (Class A
cathode-biased vs. Class AB fixed-biased); power tube characteristics (6L6 clean
headroom and extended lows/highs; EL34 aggressive upper-mid punch; EL84 chime and
early compression; 6V6 vintage rounded breakup); negative feedback loops and their
effect on damping factor and transient tightness; and Presence and Resonance
controls (variable feedback filtering).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies amplifier behavioral characteristics from audio recordings: determines
the operating class and tube family by listening to breakup texture and dynamic
stiffness; differentiates between preamp fizz and power tube crunch; and analyzes
low-end damping to determine negative feedback depth.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When an amplifier feels "stiff" or "spongy," diagnoses the circuit origin (e.g.,
solid-state diode rectifier with heavy negative feedback vs. tube rectifier with
zero negative feedback).

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Specifies complete amplifier behavior in platform-independent terms: defines preamp
gain structure, tone stack frequency response, presence feedback curve, master-volume
saturation threshold, and power supply sag depth to achieve the target dynamic feel.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how cranking the master volume will shift the primary distortion from
preamp sizzle to power-stage body and output transformer saturation; verifies that
tone stack adjustments do not cause unintended gain reduction.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Gain Staging (Domain 2): Channel volume vs. master volume balance.
  - Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Power amp damping factor directly controls
    speaker cone resonance.
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Preamp vs. power amp distortion textures.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Believing that an amplifier's tone is defined solely by its front-panel brand badge.
  - Assuming all tube amplifiers behave identically if their EQ knobs are at noon.
  - Trying to achieve power tube saturation by turning up preamp gain.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying circuit behavior, tube emulation dynamics, tone stack
    curves, and power section parameters.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): SPICE circuit simulation algorithms.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Proprietary amp block names (e.g., "Brit 8000", "Plexi 50W").

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that Marshall amps have more mids than Fender amps and 6L6 tubes are cleaner than EL34s.
  - L3: Chooses a British master-volume EL34 head with high preamp gain for an 80s rock tone.
  - L4: Diagnoses that a lead tone lacks sustain because the power section has too
        much negative feedback and high damping, recommending an open-loop Class A
        architecture with cathode-biased EL84s to introduce singing compression.
  - L5: Engineers a complete platform-independent amplifier specification: a high-gain
        4-stage triode preamp with an early cathode-follower driven FMV tone stack,
        a long-tailed pair phase inverter pushed into mild asymmetrical clipping,
        and an EL34 fixed-bias Class AB power section with variable negative feedback
        tuned to low damping, allowing the speaker's natural 110 Hz resonance to bloom
        dynamically while keeping upper-mid transients aggressive.

DOMAIN 9: LOUDSPEAKER & CABINET ENGINEERING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
The guitar loudspeaker and cabinet enclosure function as an acoustic transducer
and filter, responsible for up to 70% of the audible tone curve.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
No amount of amplifier tweaking can compensate for an inappropriate loudspeaker or
cabinet. A professional engineer must specify cabinet acoustics from physical
principles independently of commercial cabinet names.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands loudspeaker electromechanics (voice coil inductance, magnet weight,
cone paper formulation, surround damping, dust cap material and size); speaker
resonance frequency (Fs), sensitivity (dB/W/m), and power handling; cone breakup
modes (mechanical distortion at high frequencies generating harmonic coloration);
enclosure acoustic loading (closed-back infinite baffle vs. open-back dipole vs.
ported bass-reflex); enclosure volume and internal standing waves; cabinet baffle
resonance and wood material (birch ply vs. pine vs. MDF); directional dispersion
and beaming (high-frequency beaming along the central acoustic axis).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies speaker and cabinet characteristics from recorded sound: recognizes
the characteristic 2.5 kHz - 3 kHz upper-mid spike of a Celestion Vintage 30, the
woody, compressed midrange of a Greenback, or the flat, high-headroom response of
an EVM-12L; differentiates between the tight, directional punch of a closed-back
4x12 and the airy, omnidirectional bloom of an open-back 1x12.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Diagnoses whether low-end boominess is an acoustic cabinet resonance (typically
90-120 Hz) or electronic amplifier resonance; determines whether fizzy highs are
due to amplifier distortion or harsh speaker cone breakup.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Specifies the acoustic reproduction system based on musical objectives:
  - Selects closed-back 4x12 with high-power drivers for tight, focused modern metal.
  - Selects open-back 2x12 with low-efficiency, early-breakup drivers for dynamic,
    spacious vintage blues/rock.
  - Pairs contrasting drivers (e.g., V30 and G12H-75) in the same cabinet to combine
    mid-cut with extended low-end punch.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how cabinet construction changes the acoustic pressure wave arriving at
the microphone; verifies that speaker breakup adds musical warmth rather than
uncontrolled intermodulation hash.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Amplifier Behaviour (Domain 8): Amplifier damping factor interacts with speaker Fs.
  - Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Speaker cone geometry dictates microphone placement.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Cabinet acts as the primary steep low-pass filter
    above 5 kHz.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Treating cabinet selection as an afterthought compared to amplifier selection.
  - Assuming a 4x12 is always "better" or "heavier" than a 2x12 or 1x12.
  - Forcing an amplifier's EQ to extreme values to fix a fundamental mismatch in
    speaker voicing.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying enclosure type, baffle construction, driver electromechanical
    voicing, breakup thresholds, and multi-driver combinations.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Finite Element Analysis (FEA) acoustic wave modeling.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Specific commercial cabinet IR preset numbers.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that closed-back cabs have more bass and Greenbacks sound vintage.
  - L3: Pairs a high-gain head with a 4x12 closed-back cabinet loaded with Vintage 30s for metal.
  - L4: Identifies that a boxy resonance at 450 Hz is caused by an undersized 1x12
        enclosure, recommending an oversized birch-ply cabinet with higher internal
        volume to shift the resonant peak below the guitar's fundamental passband.
  - L5: Engineers an acoustic cabinet specification pairing an open-back pine cabinet
        with an alnico-magnet driver for an edge-of-breakup roots rock tone, explaining
        how the rear dipole radiation fills the room with spacious low-mids while the
        early voice-coil compression smooths out pick transients without requiring
        electronic compression.


================================================================================
DOMAIN 10: MICROPHONE & CAPTURE ENGINEERING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
In recorded music, the listener never hears the amplifier directly; they hear the
microphone's capture of the loudspeaker. The choice, position, angle, and distance
of the microphone are the recording engineer's ultimate EQ and acoustic filter.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Microphone placement is the single most critical bridge between raw guitar gear and
a finished record. A professional must reason in 3D physical and acoustic space.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands microphone transducer physics (moving-coil dynamic, ribbon, small/large
diaphragm condenser); polar patterns (cardioid, figure-8, omnidirectional) and
their off-axis frequency attenuation; proximity effect (low-frequency boost as
distance decreases in directional mics); loudspeaker acoustic radiation geometry
(center dust-cap is bright, fast, and aggressive; cone edge is warm, dark, and
sluggish); microphone angle (on-axis captures direct transient highs; off-axis
averages wave arrivals, smoothing out harshness and fizz); distance (close capture
0.5-2 cm minimizes room reflections; distant capture 10-30 cm integrates the entire
cone and introduces early reflections); multi-mic phase alignment; and ambient room
capture (boundary mics, Blumlein/spaced pairs).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Accurately reconstructs the capture environment from audio evidence: identifies
the microphone family by transient speed and frequency extension (dynamic mid-punch
vs. ribbon smooth top and deep lows vs. condenser ultra-fast transient detail);
estimates microphone distance from proximity effect weight; deduces mic angle
from the presence or absence of 5 kHz - 7 kHz harshness.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a recorded tone sounds "fizzy and detached," diagnoses whether the mic is
pointed directly at the center dust cap, or placed too far back in an untreated room.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Designs a 3D capture strategy from acoustic requirements:
  - Specifies primary mic position (Cap Edge for balanced attack; Cone for body).
  - Specifies distance to control proximity low-end weight.
  - Specifies angle (e.g., 45 degrees off-axis) to tame aggressive non-linear clipping
    fizz without introducing electronic phase artifacts.
  - Pairs complementary microphones (e.g., Dynamic 57 for bite + Ribbon 121 for
    low-mid warmth) with strict phase alignment.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts the exact acoustic curve shift resulting from a 1 cm move along the cone
radius; verifies that multi-mic combinations sum cleanly without hollow phase cancellation.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Speaker dispersion dictates microphone capture zones.
  - Phase, Polarity & Multi-Source (Domain 11): Multi-mic arrays require millisecond
    delay alignment.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Microphone placement acts as a natural, phase-pure acoustic filter.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Slapping a default dynamic mic dead-center on the cone and trying to fix the
    resulting ice-pick highs with post-EQ.
  - Ignoring distance and forgetting that moving a mic 2 cm back cuts 4 dB of sub-bass.
  - Using multiple microphones without verifying phase coherence.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Specifying transducer types, polar patterns, 3D placement (X/Y/Distance/Angle),
    and multi-mic complementary roles.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Trigonometric coordinate conversion and 3D rendering.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): IK Multimedia VIR grid coordinates or proprietary IR file formats.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that moving a mic toward the edge makes it darker and ribbons sound warmer.
  - L3: Places a dynamic mic on the cap edge at 1 inch distance for a rock rhythm tone.
  - L4: Diagnoses excessive 6 kHz fizz on a high-gain metal track as on-axis dust-cap
        beaming, angling the mic 45 degrees off-axis at the cap edge to smooth the
        highs while moving it 1 cm closer to compensate for lost proximity low-end.
  - L5: Engineers a complete dual-transducer capture array: a moving-coil dynamic
        positioned at the speaker cap edge, 1.5 cm distance, on-axis to capture fast
        transient bite and upper-mid cut; paired with a figure-8 ribbon microphone
        positioned at the outer cone edge, 3 cm distance, angled 30 degrees off-axis
        to capture rich body and smooth low-end; time-aligning the two capsules to
        within 0.05 milliseconds to eliminate comb filtering and create an immense,
        three-dimensional rhythm tone.


================================================================================
DOMAIN 11: PHASE, POLARITY & MULTI-SOURCE INTERACTION
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
When multiple sound sources, microphones, or parallel processing paths are combined,
phase interaction determines whether the combined tone is thick and powerful or
hollow and thin.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Phase cancellation cannot be cured by EQ. An engineer who does not understand phase
will mistake comb filtering for an EQ problem, compounding the defect.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands absolute polarity (in-phase vs. 180-degree inverted); frequency-dependent
phase shift; propagation delay (sound travels ~343 m/s; 1 ms delay = ~34 cm distance);
comb filtering (constructive and destructive interference creating a series of
notches at f = (2n+1) / (2 * delta_t)); phase cancellation in dual-microphone setups;
comb filtering from floor/wall boundary reflections; phase coherence across parallel
processing paths; and all-pass filters.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies phase anomalies from audio recordings: detects the classic "hollow,"
"nasal," or "distant" sound of comb filtering; recognizes when low frequencies
disappear upon summing two signals; identifies when stereo width collapses in mono
due to out-of-phase components.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a dual-mic blend sounds thin and lacks punch, tests for phase cancellation
and propagation delay before adjusting EQ; checks whether flipping polarity or
time-aligning capsules by 0.2 ms restores full low-end body.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Calculates and enforces phase coherence:
  - Inverts polarity when microphones face opposite directions (e.g., front and back
    of open-back cab).
  - Time-aligns multi-mic capsules either physically (placing diaphragms in the same
    plane) or electronically (delay compensation).
  - Deliberately uses controlled phase offsets only when a specific comb-filtered
    hollow texture is artistically desired (e.g., Boston-style notched leads).

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts the exact frequency notches that will result from a given physical mic
offset; validates mono-compatibility of all stereo and parallel guitar paths.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Multi-mic placement is the primary source of phase issues.
  - Signal Topology (Domain 7): Parallel paths must sum coherently.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Comb filtering creates severe, unfixable spectral notches.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Attempting to boost low frequencies with an EQ when two microphones are canceling
    each other out out-of-phase.
  - Assuming two microphones sound "fuller" without verifying phase alignment.
  - Forgetting that an open-back cabinet radiates rear sound in opposite polarity to the front.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Diagnosing phase defects, calculating time-alignment requirements,
    and specifying polarity.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Automatic cross-correlation delay calculation DSP.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform-specific phase switch buttons and delay buffers.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that out-of-phase microphones sound thin and cancel out bass.
  - L3: Flips the phase switch on the secondary mic to see if the bass gets louder.
  - L4: Calculates that a 2.5 cm physical offset between a dynamic and ribbon mic
        is creating a devastating phase notch at 3.4 kHz, recommending a 0.07 ms delay
        compensation to bring them into perfect coherence.
  - L5: Diagnoses a complex acoustic cancellation issue where a floor reflection is
        interfering with a close cabinet mic at 800 Hz, repositioning the cabinet
        off the reflective boundary and aligning an off-axis secondary mic to
        constructively reinforce the 800 Hz region, achieving perfect mono-compatibility
        and a massive low-mid punch.

DOMAIN 12: TIME-BASED, MODULATION & SPATIAL PROCESSING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Time-based effects (delay, reverb) and modulation (chorus, flanger, phaser) place
the guitar within an acoustic environment, create stereo width, and define the
tonal aesthetic of specific production eras.
TARGET MATURITY: L4 (Supporting)
WHY THIS TARGET:
While critical for space, depth, and vintage/modern aesthetics, time and modulation
effects are supporting elements to the core dry guitar tone. L4 provides complete
diagnostic and design capability without requiring full acoustic room simulation.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands delay mechanics (digital pristine, analog bucket-brigade device [BBD]
high-frequency loss and clock noise, tape saturation and wow/flutter); delay timing
(subdivision, tempo sync, dotted-eighth, slapback 80-140 ms, spatial pre-delay);
reverb acoustics (early reflections vs. late diffuse decay, predelay, damping,
diffusion, room size, room impulse response vs. spring reverb tank vs. plate);
modulation physics (LFO waveform, rate, depth, delay modulation, regeneration/feedback);
chorus vs. flanger (flanger uses short delays <10 ms with feedback creating comb
filtering; chorus uses delays 15-35 ms with pitch modulation); phaser (cascaded
all-pass filters creating swept notches); and stereo widening techniques (Haas effect,
pitch detuning, ping-pong).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies time-based effects in recorded audio: determines whether ambience is a
natural studio room, a spring tank, an analog plate, or digital hall; distinguishes
between tape delay warmth and digital precision; identifies modulation type, rate,
and depth; separates dry guitar core from wet reverberant tail.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a guitar track sounds "washed out," "cluttered," or "distant," diagnoses
whether the issue is excessive reverb wet mix, lack of predelay, or delay repeats
masking the dry guitar's transients.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Integrates spatial processing to serve the musical arrangement:
  - Uses short slapback or studio room ambience to give dry guitars dimension without
    cluttering a dense rock mix.
  - Filters low and high frequencies on delay repeats (Abbey Road reverb/delay trick:
    HPF at 500 Hz, LPF at 8 kHz) to prevent low-end mud and high-end sizzle.
  - Configures modulation parameters to enhance width without causing pitch instability.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how reverb decay time affects mix density; verifies that stereo widening
effects remain mono-compatible without phase cancellation.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Signal Topology (Domain 7): Placement of delays/reverbs post-gain to preserve clarity.
  - Musical & Production Context (Domain 14): Reverb/delay choices must match historical era.
  - Psychoacoustics & Perception (Domain 13): Predelay and early reflections dictate perceived depth.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Drowning a poorly matched guitar tone in heavy reverb to hide defects.
  - Running long, un-synced delays that clash with the rhythm section's groove.
  - Failing to filter the low end of a reverb, creating an uncontrollable low-mid boom in the mix.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Designing spatial depth, selecting delay/reverb architectures, tuning
    timing, and configuring modulation.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Reverb DSP algorithm generation and convolution math.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Specific commercial pedal emulation presets.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that chorus makes things sound 80s and delay adds repeats.
  - L3: Sets a quarter-note delay with low feedback in the effects loop for lead solos.
  - L4: Diagnoses that a lead guitar disappears in a heavy mix because the reverb has
        zero predelay and a long 3.5s decay; introduces 45 ms of predelay, cuts the
        decay to 1.8s, and high-passes the wet return at 600 Hz, allowing pick
        transients to remain upfront while the tail creates massive spatial depth.


================================================================================
DOMAIN 13: PSYCHOACOUSTICS & PERCEPTION
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Human hearing is non-linear. An engineer who does not understand perceptual
mechanisms will fall victim to loudness bias, misjudge midrange presence, and make
ineffective processing decisions.
TARGET MATURITY: L4 (Supporting)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Psychoacoustics provides the perceptual lens through which all engineering evidence
must be evaluated. L4 ensures the engineer avoids perceptual traps, compensates for
ear fatigue, and understands how listeners perceive loudness, width, and depth.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands equal-loudness contours (Fletcher-Munson curves: hearing sensitivity
peaks between 2 kHz - 5 kHz; bass and treble perception drops at low SPL);
loudness bias (louder signals are perceived as having better bass, clearer highs,
and superior punch); frequency masking (louder sounds in a critical band mask
quieter sounds within that band); temporal masking (forward and backward masking
of transients); phantom center imaging and binaural localization (inter-aural time
differences [ITD] and inter-aural level differences [ILD]); and the precedence
(Haas) effect.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Compensates for perceptual biases when evaluating audio: ensures all A/B comparisons
are strictly RMS/LUFS level-matched to eliminate loudness deception; identifies
when a guitar sounds "thin" merely because it is auditioned at low volume; and
recognizes when masking from bass or cymbals is making a guitar seem dark.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a user complains that a tone "sounds dull in the mix but harsh when soloed,"
diagnoses the psychoacoustic cause: masking in the midrange from other instruments
forcing an over-compensation in the 4 kHz - 6 kHz region.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Applies perceptual principles to sound design:
  - Carves out complementary spectral pockets rather than boosting guitar frequencies.
  - Uses the Haas effect (10-25 ms delay) carefully for width while checking mono
    collapse.
  - Focuses EQ adjustments on the critical midrange (800 Hz - 3 kHz) where the ear
    is most sensitive to intelligibility.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how a tone will translate across different listening environments (headphones
vs. nearfield monitors vs. mobile speakers); verifies that perceived punch is
achieved through transient contrast rather than raw loudness.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Gain Staging (Domain 2): Loudness matching is mandatory for objective comparison.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Critical bands and frequency masking dictate EQ needs.
  - Reference Analysis (Domain 15): Isolating perceptual guitar weight from the full mix.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Evaluating EQ or amp changes at different volume levels and concluding the louder one is "better."
  - Mixing in solo and expecting the tone to sit perfectly within the full arrangement.
  - Over-boosting 10 kHz "air" on electric guitars, fatiguing the listener's ears.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Applying perceptual principles to evaluation, level matching, and
    spatial/spectral design.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): ITU-R BS.1770 LUFS calculation algorithms.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Operating system audio output volume settings.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that louder sounds seem better and human ears hear mids best.
  - L3: Matches volume before comparing two different amp presets.
  - L4: Recognizes that a client's request for "more bite" is an illusion caused by
        cymbals masking the 3 kHz region; rather than boosting 3 kHz on the guitar
        (which causes harshness), introduces a dynamic notch at 3 kHz on the cymbals,
        revealing the guitar's natural bite without altering its tone.


================================================================================
DOMAIN 14: MUSICAL & PRODUCTION CONTEXT
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Guitar tone does not exist in a vacuum. It serves an arrangement, a genre, an era,
and a production aesthetic.
TARGET MATURITY: L4 (Supporting)
WHY THIS TARGET:
An engineer must understand stylistic conventions and historical recording practices
to interpret user intent accurately. L4 provides deep historical and arrangement
reasoning while strictly avoiding dogmatic, recipe-driven thinking.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands historical recording practices across eras (1960s British invasion
analog tube desks and ribbon mics; 1970s multi-track tape, high-volume Marshall
stacks, dynamic mics; 1980s rack processors, solid-state clipping, digital delays,
SSL consoles; 1990s raw grunge, Rectifiers, analog revival; 2000s modern metal,
digital modelers, surgical multi-tracking); arrangement density (power trios vs.
dense multi-layered arrangements); guitar roles (rhythm foundation, lead cut,
ambient texture); tracking techniques (single-track, double-track hard-panned,
quad-tracking); and genre aesthetics (blues, classic rock, thrash, djent, shoegaze).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Uses style and era as contextual evidence, not as a rigid recipe: analyzes reference
tracks to deduce tracking density (detecting the microscopic timing differences of
double-tracking vs. artificial stereo chorusing); infers the overall production aesthetic
(dry, bone-naked 70s rock vs. polished, wet 80s production).

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a recreation sounds "wrong," evaluates whether the discrepancy is due to
arrangement differences (e.g., trying to achieve a wall-of-sound rhythm tone with a
single centered guitar track when the reference is quad-tracked).

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Adapts tone design to fit the musical role and arrangement:
  - Trims low end and narrows spatial width on rhythm guitars in dense mixes to leave
    space for bass and kick drum.
  - Designs a full-range, warm, dynamic tone for a solo guitar or power trio.
  - Uses era-appropriate signal processing choices to maintain stylistic authenticity.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how double-tracking will change the perceived low-end weight and stereo
spread; ensures that the designed tone fulfills its exact function in the mix.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Signal Topology (Domain 7): Tracking and spatial layout define signal routing.
  - Dynamics & Envelope (Domain 5): Staccato modern metal vs. loose vintage blues dynamics.
  - Intervention Selection (Domain 17): Knowing when historical simplicity outperforms modern complexity.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Treating genre as a rigid gear recipe (e.g., "All 80s metal must use a JCM800 and a TS9").
  - Designing a massive, soloed guitar tone that completely overwhelms the bass and drums.
  - Confusing double-tracked rhythm guitars with a stereo chorus effect.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Interpreting musical role, arrangement density, and historical production practices.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): DAW track layout and multi-track stem management.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Downloading artist-name branded presets.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that 70s rock used Marshalls and 80s metal used lots of gain and chorus.
  - L3: Suggests a dry, mid-heavy tone with no reverb for a 1970s classic rock rhythm track.
  - L4: Analyzes an early 1990s grunge reference and deduces that the massive, raw
        rhythm tone was achieved not by high amplifier gain, but by layering an
        overdriven tube amp with a gritty solid-state fuzz pedal, doubled and panned
        hard left and right with dry, bone-close dynamic mic capture.

DOMAIN 15: REFERENCE ANALYSIS & TONE MATCHING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Tone matching is the central operational task of Tone Translator. Comparing a target
reference with a user's tone requires deep analytical deconstruction across multiple
acoustic dimensions.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Tone matching cannot be solved by superficial EQ curve matching. A true Sound
Engineer must isolate individual causal factors (gain, dynamics, mic placement,
speaker voicing) from complex audio evidence.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands comparative acoustic analysis across eight distinct dimensions:
  1. Spectral Balance (frequency distribution, resonances, tilt).
  2. Gain Architecture & Saturation (clipping depth, harmonic symmetry, texture).
  3. Dynamic Envelope (transient snap, compression depth, sustain bloom).
  4. Non-linear Compression & Sag (touch responsiveness, dynamic collapse).
  5. Loudspeaker & Cabinet Voicing (low-end damping, mid focus, cone breakup).
  6. Acoustic Capture Geometry (mic type, proximity weight, off-axis angle, room ratio).
  7. Noise & Artifact Profile (hiss, hum, gate artifacts).
  8. Spatial & Temporal Field (stereo width, delay reflections, reverb decay).
Understands the fundamental rule of tone matching: AN OBSERVED DIFFERENCE DOES NOT
AUTOMATICALLY ESTABLISH ITS CAUSE.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Deconstructs complex reference audio into constituent physical components:
separates guitar tone from bass guitar, drums, and mix mastering; isolates direct
transients from reverberant tails; distinguishes between an EQ difference and a
saturation difference; recognizes when a reference contains multi-tracked guitars.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a target tone has more high-end energy than the current tone, rigorously
diagnoses whether the difference is:
  (a) higher amplifier treble/presence settings,
  (b) more aggressive harmonic saturation generating new upper frequencies,
  (c) an on-axis condenser mic vs. an off-axis dynamic mic, or
  (d) brighter guitar pickups.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Constructs a ranked, prioritized intervention plan based on root causes:
  - Targets the largest acoustic discrepancies first (typically cabinet/mic and gain staging).
  - Never uses a downstream tool to fix an upstream discrepancy.
  - Level-matches reference and current audio to ensure valid comparisons.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Predicts how each planned intervention will narrow the multi-dimensional distance
to the target; measures objective deltas (crest factor, spectral centroid, harmonic
distortion) to verify convergence toward the reference.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Integrates ALL domains (1 through 14).
  - Diagnosis & Root-Cause (Domain 16): Feeds directly into causal diagnosis.
  - Iterative Engineering (Domain 21): Provides the comparison metric for each iteration.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Running an automated "match EQ" plugin across the master and calling it a matched tone.
  - Mistaking the bass guitar's low end for the guitar's cabinet resonance.
  - Concluding that two tones match simply because their static frequency response
    curves look similar on a spectrum analyzer.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Multi-dimensional comparative analysis, causal delta deduction,
    and priority planning.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) DSP algorithms and
    audio decoders.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform-specific preset import/export tools.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Can hear that the reference has more distortion and more treble than the user tone.
  - L3: Increases gain and treble on the amp model to get closer to the reference.
  - L4: Determines that the reference tone's brightness is not top-end EQ, but 3rd-harmonic
        saturation produced by a pushed phase inverter, choosing to push the master
        volume rather than boosting amplifier treble.
  - L5: Deconstructs a dense commercial mix reference, identifying that the perceived
        heavy rhythm tone consists of two quad-tracked guitars with surprisingly low
        gain (edge-of-crunch), aggressive 120 Hz pre-filtering, a closed-back 4x12
        with V30 drivers captured by an off-axis dynamic mic at 2 cm, and an acute
        mid-cut at 500 Hz leaving space for the bass guitar; successfully designs a
        single-track profile that captures the identical harmonic feel, transient bite,
        and mix pocket.

DOMAIN 16: DIAGNOSIS & ROOT-CAUSE REASONING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
In audio engineering, symptoms are often separated from causes by multiple stages.
A professional engineer must diagnose causes rather than merely suppressing symptoms.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Treating symptoms (e.g., cutting treble with an EQ when a diode clipper is generating
harsh harmonics) introduces phase distortion and leaves the underlying defect intact.
L5 diagnostic rigor is mandatory.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands the structured diagnostic method:
  Observation -> Candidate Causes -> Supporting Evidence -> Conflicting Evidence
  -> Ranked Causal Hypotheses -> Targeted Intervention -> Validation.
Understands multi-factor causation (multiple interacting mechanisms producing a
single acoustic symptom); symptom masking (one severe defect hiding another); and
downstream vs. upstream causality.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Actively searches for conflicting evidence that disproves an initial assumption;
distinguishes between correlated symptoms and causal drivers; refuses to jump to
a conclusion based on a single acoustic cue.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Executes rigorous causal analysis:
  - Symptom: "Harsh, unmusical high-frequency fizz."
  - Candidate Cause 1: Bright guitar pickups. (Conflicting evidence: clean tone is warm).
  - Candidate Cause 2: Power amp crossover distortion. (Conflicting evidence: amp is Class A).
  - Candidate Cause 3: On-axis microphone placement capturing dust-cap resonance.
    (Supporting evidence: fizz is concentrated at 5.5 kHz with narrow beaming).
  - Validated Hypothesis: Cause 3.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Selects interventions that directly address the validated root cause at its
origin, avoiding secondary compensatory processing downstream.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Validates the diagnostic hypothesis by observing whether the targeted intervention
cured the symptom without creating unintended side effects; immediately re-evaluates
the hypothesis if the intervention fails.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Causal location of spectral defects.
  - Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Distinguishing harmonic distortion from linear EQ.
  - Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Physical acoustic capture vs. electronic defects.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Treating the symptom where it is heard rather than where it is generated.
  - Assuming the first possible cause that comes to mind is the true cause.
  - Applying multiple simultaneous changes so that the true cause can never be isolated.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Causal hypothesis formation, evidence weighting, root-cause isolation,
    and diagnostic reasoning.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Automated rule-engine execution trees.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Specific platform debugging console tools.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that bad sound can come from bad settings anywhere in the chain.
  - L3: When a tone sounds muddy, turns down the bass knob on the amp.
  - L4: When a tone sounds muddy, checks whether the mud is in the input signal,
        preamp distortion, cabinet resonance, or mic proximity before changing settings.
  - L5: Faced with a complex complaint that "the tone falls apart during heavy chords,"
        systematically evaluates pickup intermodulation, power supply sag, phase
        inverter blocking distortion, and cabinet acoustic loading; isolates the
        root cause to blocking distortion in the second gain stage caused by excessive
        sub-100 Hz signal; cures it permanently by inserting a 6 dB/oct high-pass
        filter at 130 Hz before the first tube, restoring crystal-clear chord
        separation without sacrificing final low-end fullness.

DOMAIN 17: INTERVENTION SELECTION & MINIMALISM
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Every additional processing block introduces phase shift, latency, noise, and
potential artifacts. Professional engineering is defined by achieving the target
sound with the minimum necessary intervention.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Amateur tone design is characterized by chains bloated with redundant EQs, compressors,
and boosters. A professional engineer knows how to make existing core stages do the
work, eliminating gratuitous complexity.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands the Principle of Minimal Intervention; the law of diminishing returns
in audio processing; accumulated phase distortion through cascading filters;
processor efficiency (adjusting an existing amplifier tone stack vs. adding a 10-band
graphic EQ); the acoustic purity of physical solutions (adjusting microphone placement)
over electronic patches; and when "doing nothing" is the most professional choice.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Evaluates whether a perceived acoustic discrepancy actually requires intervention,
or whether it falls within acceptable musical tolerances and human hearing variation;
recognizes when an existing stage already has the capability to solve the issue.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Analyzes proposed signal chains for redundant, competing, or self-canceling
processors (e.g., an EQ boosting 2 kHz followed immediately by an EQ cutting 2 kHz;
a compressor fighting an upstream expander).

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Applies a strict intervention hierarchy:
  1. Optimize the primary electro-acoustic source (amplifier, cabinet, microphone).
  2. Adjust existing onboard controls (tone stack, presence, gain knobs).
  3. Re-position or change the physical capture device (microphone).
  4. Only if stages 1-3 cannot physically achieve the goal, insert a dedicated outboard
     processor (pre-filter, rack EQ, transparent limiter).
  5. Never insert a processor without an explicit, documented causal objective.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Verifies that removing a processor does not degrade the core tone; validates that
the final design achieves maximum sonic authority with the leanest possible architecture.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Signal Topology (Domain 7): Prevents chain bloat and unnecessary serial stages.
  - Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Eliminates redundant cascading EQ blocks.
  - Trade-Off & Constraint (Domain 18): Balances intervention simplicity against perfection.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Inserting an EQ pedal, a rack EQ, and a cab EQ all in the same signal path.
  - Adding a new processor to solve every perceived imperfection.
  - Assuming that a complex, 12-block signal chain is inherently more "professional"
    than a 3-block chain.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Selecting minimal intervention pathways, eliminating redundant blocks,
    and enforcing processing discipline.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Automated removal of duplicate blocks in serializer code.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform maximum block limits (e.g., AT5's 4-stomp limit).

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that too many pedals can add noise and degrade tone.
  - L3: Removes a chorus and second delay pedal that aren't being used in a rock preset.
  - L4: Refuses to add a post-cab parametric EQ to remove a 4 kHz spike, achieving
        the identical result by turning the physical microphone 15 degrees off-axis,
        preserving phase purity and eliminating a processor.
  - L5: Takes a bloated 10-block preset (noise gate, tube screamer, 10-band EQ, amp,
        chorus, delay, reverb, parametric EQ, limiter, exciter) and consolidates it
        into an ultra-pure, authoritative 3-block signal design (vintage-voiced boost,
        cranked master-volume tube amp, dual phase-aligned mic capture), achieving
        vastly superior punch, 12 dB lower noise, and flawless touch dynamics.

DOMAIN 18: TRADE-OFF & CONSTRAINT REASONING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
In audio engineering, there are no solutions—only trade-offs. Increasing gain adds
sustain but destroys transients and increases noise; cutting bass tightens punch
but loses warmth; angling a mic smooths fizz but softens attack.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
An engineer who does not explicitly account for trade-offs will produce unbalanced,
fragile tones. Professional competence requires conscious, deliberate balancing
of competing acoustic priorities.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands fundamental audio engineering trade-offs:
  - Saturation vs. Transient Definition (more compression/harmonics vs. less pick punch).
  - Low-Frequency Tightness vs. Low-End Weight/Body (HPF tightening vs. thinness).
  - High-Frequency Clarity vs. Harshness/Fizz (air and bite vs. ear fatigue).
  - Noise Gating vs. Natural Note Decay (clean silence vs. chopped tails).
  - Acoustic Ambience vs. In-Your-Face Intelligibility (spacious depth vs. mix wash).
  - Dynamic Range vs. Consistent Density (touch expression vs. wall-of-sound power).
Understands physical constraints (headroom limits, speaker power handling, phase
coherence bounds, acoustic room boundaries).

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Identifies the hidden acoustic cost of any proposed setting: evaluates what was
sacrificed when an improvement was made; detects when an aggressive intervention
has compromised a secondary musical attribute.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When a tone is improved in one dimension but degraded in another (e.g., "The rhythm
is tighter now, but single-note solos sound thin and weak"), diagnoses the exact
trade-off mechanism responsible.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Makes explicit, documented trade-off decisions:
  - Determines which sonic attribute is paramount for the specific musical role.
  - Calibrates settings to the optimal balance point where maximum benefit is
    obtained with minimum acceptable sacrifice.
  - Clearly articulates to the user what was traded away and why.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Validates both sides of the trade-off ledger after an intervention: verifies that
the primary goal was achieved AND that the secondary cost remained within acceptable
bounds.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Gain Staging (Domain 2): Headroom vs. noise vs. distortion depth.
  - Dynamics & Envelope (Domain 5): Transient punch vs. sustain.
  - Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Direct bite vs. smooth off-axis body.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Believing that an intervention has "zero downside."
  - Chasing an extreme attribute (maximum gain, razor-sharp brightness) without
    evaluating what was destroyed in the process.
  - Ignoring client constraints (e.g., requiring a pristine clean tone and a crushing
    lead tone from a single-channel amp without channel switching).

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Formulating trade-off matrices, balancing competing goals, and
    making informed compromises.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Automated constraint satisfaction math solvers.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform CPU usage limits.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that adding gain also adds hiss.
  - L3: Decides to accept a little bit of noise in order to get enough sustain for a solo.
  - L4: Evaluates pre-distortion bass roll-off: explicitly weighs the trade-off
        between tight palm-mute articulation and low-end rumble, tuning the high-pass
        filter to 110 Hz to maintain punch while eliminating sub-bass flub.
  - L5: Manages a multi-dimensional trade-off matrix for a complex progressive metal
        rhythm tone: balances extreme high-gain saturation against transient attack,
        sacrificing 1.5 dB of gross sustain to preserve pick definition, compensating
        with a 45-degree off-axis microphone placement that softens the resulting
        diode harshness, and clearly documenting why this compromise delivers a
        superior mix-ready result over an unconstrained high-gain preset.

DOMAIN 19: UNCERTAINTY & EVIDENCE DISCIPLINE
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Real-world audio evidence is incomplete, noisy, and ambiguous. A professional
engineer must operate with strict epistemic discipline—knowing what is known,
what is inferred, and when evidence is simply insufficient.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
Hallucinating certainty from ambiguous data produces disastrous engineering decisions.
An AI Sound Engineer must avoid false precision and communicate confidence with
absolute honesty.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands epistemic categorization of engineering information:
  - Measured Evidence (objective data: verified RMS, spectrum, crest factor).
  - Observed Evidence (audible phenomena confirmed by listening).
  - Inferred Evidence (deductions based on acoustic principles: e.g., inferring
    an open-back cab from rear reflection cues).
  - Assumed Evidence (working premises that lack direct proof).
  - Supplied Context (user text, declared genre, artist bio).
  - Unknown Information (parameters that cannot be determined from available data).
Understands the danger of false precision (e.g., claiming a mic is at 1.414 cm distance
or confidence is 98.7% based on low-resolution MP3 audio); understands when evidence
is insufficient to select a unique solution.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Rigidly segregates evidence categories in analysis records: never presents an
inference as an observed fact; flags assumptions explicitly; identifies when audio
quality (low bitrate, background noise, heavy mastering) obscures guitar details.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Recognizes when multiple contradictory hypotheses are equally supported by available
evidence; refuses to invent certainty; states clearly: "Available evidence supports
both an overdriven Tweed combo and an overdriven EL84 head; cannot differentiate
without isolated stem."

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Calibrates decisions to the confidence level of the evidence:
  - When evidence is robust, proceeds with targeted, high-precision interventions.
  - When evidence is incomplete, selects robust, generalized solutions that perform
    well across multiple plausible scenarios.
  - Asks clarifying questions or requests missing evidence (e.g., asking for an
    isolated guitar stem or guitar pickup details) when critical branches diverge.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Continuously tests assumptions against new evidence; lowers confidence ratings
when outcomes deviate from expectations.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Reference Analysis (Domain 15): Differentiating guitar tone from mix context.
  - Diagnosis & Root-Cause (Domain 16): Separating supported hypotheses from speculation.
  - Alternative Solutions (Domain 20): High uncertainty requires exploring alternatives.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Emitting an arbitrary "95% confidence" score when operating on a vague text prompt.
  - Guessing exact historical gear models from a 30-second YouTube audio clip with certainty.
  - Concealing uncertainty behind technical-sounding jargon.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Categorizing evidence, calculating epistemically calibrated confidence,
    and declaring insufficient evidence.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Bayesian probability math libraries.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform telemetry error codes.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that it's hard to hear the guitar clearly when drums and bass are loud.
  - L3: Admits that they cannot tell what kind of amp was used on a low-quality recording.
  - L4: In analyzing a reference track, explicitly categorizes: "Observed: strong
        3 kHz peak and fast compression; Inferred: closed-back 4x12 and dynamic mic;
        Unknown: whether pickup is active or passive; Confidence: 65%."
  - L5: Given an ambiguous, heavily mastered reference, declares that evidence is
        insufficient to distinguish between an overdriven fuzz pedal into a clean amp
        versus a cranked vintage tube amp; rather than guessing, designs a primary
        solution with documented high uncertainty, formulates an alternative branch,
        and provides the user with an exact diagnostic test (checking touch sensitivity
        when guitar volume is rolled back) to definitively isolate the truth.

DOMAIN 20: ALTERNATIVE SOLUTION REASONING
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
In creative audio engineering, there is rarely only one "right" way to achieve a
sonic goal. A professional must be capable of generating and evaluating multiple
distinct, defensible solutions.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
An engineer locked into a single path cannot adapt when constraints (such as
available gear, CPU limits, or player preference) block that path. L5 competence
requires multi-solution agility.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands acoustic equivalence and alternative intervention pathways:
  - Upstream vs. Downstream Equivalence (achieving brightness via pickup tone pot
    vs. overdrive pedal tone vs. amp presence vs. mic placement vs. post-EQ).
  - Electromechanical vs. Electronic Equivalence (achieving low-end tightness via
    a closed-back cabinet vs. an upstream high-pass filter).
  - Single-Chain vs. Parallel-Chain alternatives.
Understands comparative evaluation criteria: tone authenticity, dynamic feel,
simplicity, noise floor, and robustness across different playing styles.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Recognizes when a problem permits multiple valid architectural approaches; identifies
the distinct pros and cons of each pathway based on evidence and user constraints.

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
Evaluates competing solution paths for a single diagnosed root cause:
  - Path A (The Traditionalist): Preamp overdrive + closed-back 4x12 + dynamic mic.
  - Path B (The Modernist): Clean high-headroom amp + multi-stage distortion pedal
    + studio IR capture + post-EQ notch.
  - Compares both on transient punch, noise, and ease of control.

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Develops and presents viable, professionally defensible alternatives:
  - Recommends a primary solution based on optimal balance of objectives.
  - Provides a fully articulated alternative solution when user preferences, gear
    limitations, or playing contexts warrant it.
  - Explains the specific acoustic trade-offs between the alternatives.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Can switch to an alternative solution cleanly if the primary solution fails outcome
evaluation; compares real-world results of alternative paths.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Trade-Off & Constraint (Domain 18): Alternatives embody different trade-off balances.
  - Signal Topology (Domain 7): Alternatives often use different routing structures.
  - Intervention Selection (Domain 17): Compares minimalist vs. comprehensive pathways.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - Believing that there is only one "holy grail" signal chain for any given tone.
  - Offering "fake alternatives" that are merely cosmetic variations of the same chain.
  - Abandoning engineering principles when offering an alternative.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Formulating multiple distinct engineering strategies and comparing
    their physical and acoustic consequences.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Managing UI variation dropdown menus and state.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Platform-specific preset duplication tools.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that you can get distortion from either a pedal or an amp.
  - L3: Offers a choice between using an overdrive pedal or turning up the amp's gain.
  - L4: Formulates two distinct solutions to tighten a loose low end: Solution 1
        (Pre-gain high-pass filtering via a clean boost) to preserve amp character;
        Solution 2 (Switching from an open-back to a closed-back cabinet) to solve

  - L5: Designs two fully realized, master-grade signal strategies for a complex
        recording session: Strategy A (A pure vintage analog path using a jumped
        Plexi, power attenuator, and dual-mic capture on a 4x12) delivering maximum
        touch-sensitive dynamics at the cost of higher noise; and Strategy B (A modern
        precision path using a high-gain master-volume head, transparent pre-clamp,
        and single-point ribbon capture with post-rack correction) delivering surgical
        mix consistency and zero noise; provides rigorous comparative justification
        enabling the producer to choose based on the arrangement's needs.

DOMAIN 21: ITERATIVE ENGINEERING & OUTCOME EVALUATION
================================================================================
MISSION RELEVANCE:
Real-world audio engineering is an empirical, iterative discipline. An engineer
rarely achieves perfection on the first pass; success depends on the ability to
evaluate outcomes objectively, test against expectations, and refine the design.
TARGET MATURITY: L5 (Core)
WHY THIS TARGET:
An open-loop system that emits a preset and walks away is not an engineer. Tone
Translator's ultimate value lies in its ability to refine, adjust, and converge
on a target tone through closed-loop evaluation.

KNOWLEDGE STANDARD:
Understands the empirical engineering cycle:
  Analyse -> Hypothesise -> Intervene -> Observe Result -> Compare to Target
  -> Evaluate Deltas -> Refine Hypothesis or Intervention.
Understands objective evaluation metrics (spectral difference curves, dynamic crest
factor changes, total harmonic distortion [THD], noise floor shifts); understands
the danger of "confirmation bias" (assuming an intervention worked without listening
critically); understands when an unexpected result indicates a flawed initial hypothesis.

EVIDENCE INTERPRETATION STANDARD:
Compares the modified audio output against the original baseline and the reference
target: detects whether the intervention moved the tone closer to or further from
the goal; identifies unintended side effects (e.g., the low end got tighter, but
the top end became brittle).

DIAGNOSTIC STANDARD:
When an intervention fails to produce the expected outcome, DOES NOT SIMPLY INCREASE
THE SETTING. Reconsiders the underlying hypothesis:
  - "I boosted treble 4 dB to add bite, but the tone only got harsher, not clearer.
    Therefore, the lack of bite is NOT a high-frequency EQ deficit; it is an
    absence of pick transient attack in the compressor."

DECISION-MAKING STANDARD:
Iterates with surgical discipline:
  - Changes only one variable at a time when diagnosing failures.
  - Reverts unsuccessful interventions rather than piling new corrections on top.
  - Converges systematically on the target tone across multiple iterative cycles.

OUTCOME-REASONING STANDARD:
Defines explicit verification criteria BEFORE applying an intervention; verifies
that every change achieves its intended acoustic metric without collateral damage.

REQUIRED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS:
  - Reference Analysis (Domain 15): Provides the objective delta measurements.
  - Diagnosis & Root-Cause (Domain 16): Revises causal hypotheses when interventions fail.
  - Professional Behaviour (Domain 22): Enforces empirical discipline over dogmatism.

PROFESSIONAL ANTI-PATTERNS:
  - "If it didn't work, turn the knob further in the same direction."
  - Making five changes simultaneously and not knowing which one helped or hurt.
  - Declaring success without listening critically to the resulting audio.

BOUNDARIES:
  - In Scope: Designing closed-loop refinement plans, evaluating objective acoustic
    deltas, and revising hypotheses.
  - Out of Scope (Deterministic): Automated test runner execution loops.
  - Out of Scope (Platform): Preset export and re-import mechanics.

MATURITY PROGRESSION:
  - L2: Knows that you have to listen to the change after you turn a knob.
  - L3: Listens to an EQ change, decides it's still not bright enough, and turns it up more.
  - L4: Evaluates an intervention after cutting 250 Hz to remove mud; observes that
        the mud decreased but the guitar lost its body; adjusts the filter Q to be
        narrower to preserve 180 Hz punch while removing the 250 Hz build-up.
  - L5: Executes a full closed-loop refinement cycle: establishes a baseline delta
        against a target lead tone; hypothesizes that lack of sustain is due to low
        power amp drive; increases master volume by 3 dB; observes that sustain improved
        by only 5% while lower-mid mud increased by 20%; immediately rejects the
        hypothesis, rolls back the master volume, formulates a new hypothesis targeting
        optical compressor release timing, shortens the release to 80 ms, and achieves
        a 40% increase in smooth sustain with zero low-end degradation.

SECTION 6 — CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING STANDARD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 22 competency domains cannot operate as isolated silos. Electric guitar tone
is an electro-acoustic continuum. Below are nine representative guitar tone problems
illustrating the type of CROSS-DOMAIN causal reasoning required of a professional
Sound Engineer.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 1: "Palm mutes are flubby, boomy, and lack tightness."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Turn down the bass knob on the amplifier, or insert a noise gate.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  The engineer investigates the complete causal chain from pick to ear:
  1. Source (Domain 1): Is the player muting directly over the bridge saddles, or
     too far forward? Is the guitar tuned down (e.g., Drop C), causing floppy string
     vibration and excessive fundamental energy?
  2. Gain Staging & Headroom (Domain 2): Is excessive low-frequency voltage overdriving
     the input stage of the first tube, causing grid blocking distortion?
  3. Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Where is the low end entering? If bass is cut
     pre-distortion, the distortion will be tight and articulate; if cut post-distortion,
     the distortion remains flabby, only quieter.
  4. Amplifier Behaviour (Domain 8): Is the amplifier's power section under-damped
     (low negative feedback) causing the speaker to ring out of control at its
     resonant frequency?
  5. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Is an open-back cabinet allowing out-of-control
     cone excursion, or is a closed-back 4x12 resonant peak accumulating at 110 Hz?
  6. Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Is a directional microphone placed 0.5 cm
     from the grille, creating a massive 8 dB proximity effect bass boost below 150 Hz?
  SYNTHESIS:
  The engineer formulates a multi-stage solution: applies an 18 dB/oct high-pass
  filter at 110 Hz PRE-distortion to prevent tube blocking, increases amplifier
  negative feedback slightly to tighten cone damping, and pulls the microphone back
  from 0.5 cm to 2.5 cm to naturally eliminate proximity flub.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 2: "Distortion is harsh, buzzy, and fizzy in the top end."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Turn down the treble and presence knobs, or slap a low-pass filter at 5 kHz.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Is the harshness coming from symmetrical hard
     diode clipping generating high odd-order harmonics (7th, 9th, 11th)?
  2. Gain Staging (Domain 2): Is a solid-state pedal or digital buffer clipping
     unintentionally due to poor inter-stage headroom?
  3. Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Is there excessive 3 kHz - 5 kHz energy feeding
     the clipping stage, exaggerating harsh harmonic generation?
  4. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Is the speaker cone suffering from uncontrolled
     breakup modes, or is it a high-headroom neutral speaker (like an EVM) that
     fails to roll off frequencies above 5 kHz?
  5. Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Is the microphone pointed dead-center at the
     speaker dust-cap, capturing the direct on-axis acoustic beam where high-frequency
     fizz concentrates?
  SYNTHESIS:
  Cutting treble post-amp removes air and leaves the tone dull, while the buzz
  remains. The engineer shifts the microphone 15 degrees off-axis toward the cap edge
  (Domain 10), allowing the speaker's natural acoustic dispersion to filter out the
  fizz smoothly without phase smearing, while switching an upstream hard-clipper to
  a softer asymmetrical stage (Domain 3).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 3: "Tone lacks pick attack and note definition."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Boost treble at 3 kHz, or turn up the gain for more sustain.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Source & Performance (Domain 1): Is the pick too thin (<0.7 mm) or flexing?
     Is the player using the round edge of the pick instead of the sharp tip?
  2. Dynamics & Envelope (Domain 5): Has an upstream compressor pedal squashed the
     initial 15 ms transient? Is its attack set to 5 ms instead of 30 ms?
  3. Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Is preamp gain set so high that the amplifier
     is in hard saturation, dynamically compressing the pick attack down to the
     level of the sustain?
  4. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Is a sluggish, heavy speaker cone smoothing
     out the attack?
  SYNTHESIS:
  Boosting 3 kHz on an over-saturated amp only adds harsh white noise. The engineer
  lowers the preamp gain by 20% to restore 6 dB of dynamic crest factor (Domain 2 & 3),
  and lengthens compressor attack to 35 ms (Domain 5), allowing the natural pick
  snap to slice through before saturation clamps down.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 4: "Guitar sounds huge in solo, but completely disappears in the mix."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Make the guitar louder in the mix.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): A soloed guitar tone often has scooped mids
     and massive low/high end ("the bedroom tone"). In a mix, the bass guitar masks
     its low end, cymbals mask its high end, and with no midrange, it vanishes.
  2. Psychoacoustics & Perception (Domain 13): Frequency masking in the 400 Hz - 2 kHz
     region. The human ear relies on midrange for vocal and instrument localization.
  3. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Is the cabinet heavily scooped (e.g., G12T-75
     drivers) rather than midrange-forward (e.g., Vintage 30 or Greenback)?
  4. Musical & Production Context (Domain 14): In a dense arrangement with keys and
     bass, the guitar's functional role is narrow-band percussive rhythm.
  SYNTHESIS:
  The engineer strips the scooped "bedroom" EQ, boosts 800 Hz to 1.6 kHz by 3 dB
  on the amplifier tone stack, rolls off sub-120 Hz lows to clear space for the bass,
  and cuts the reverb wet mix in half. The soloed tone sounds mid-heavy, but when the
  mix drops in, the guitar sits with razor-sharp authority.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 5: "Dual-microphone setup sounds hollow, nasal, and weak."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Boost midrange and bass with an EQ.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Phase, Polarity & Multi-Source (Domain 11): Comb filtering. If Mic 1 and Mic 2
     are at different distances from the speaker cone, the time arrival difference
     delta_t creates destructive interference notches across the spectrum.
  2. Microphone & Capture (Domain 10): Are the capsules physically aligned in the
     same plane? Is one mic picking up an out-of-phase rear reflection from an
     open-back cabinet?
  3. Frequency & Spectral (Domain 4): Nasal hollow character is the acoustic textbook
     signature of a comb-filter notch in the 500 Hz - 1 kHz region.
  SYNTHESIS:
  The engineer mutes Mic 2 and verifies that Mic 1 sounds full and punchy. When Mic 2
  is unmuted, bass vanishes. The engineer inverts polarity on Mic 2; if bass returns
  partially, the engineer then adjusts the delay alignment in 0.05 ms increments
  until the signals sum with full, phase-coherent low-mid power.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 6: "The reference tone sounds wider, deeper, and more three-dimensional."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Add a stereo chorus pedal, or put a big stereo reverb at the end.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Musical & Production Context (Domain 14): Is the reference double-tracked?
     True double-tracking (two distinct performances panned hard left and right)
     creates natural micro-timing and micro-pitch pitch differences that cannot be
     replicated by a mono guitar with a stereo effect.
  2. Time-Based, Modulation & Spatial (Domain 12): Does the reference use early
     reflections from a live room (predelay 20 ms, room decay 0.8s) rather than
     a synthetic reverb tail?
  3. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Does the reference combine two contrasting
     speaker voicings panned in stereo?
  SYNTHESIS:
  Adding a chorus pedal to a mono track creates a 1980s cheesy modulation and causes
  mono phase cancellation. The engineer identifies that the reference is double-tracked;
  designs two complementary tone profiles (one slightly darker and mid-focused, one
  brighter and tighter) designed to be tracked separately and panned left and right,
  embedding room reflections with 25 ms predelay for spatial depth.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 7: "High-gain modern metal rhythm is excessively noisy with loud idle hiss."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Slap a hard noise gate on the front and crank the threshold.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Gain Staging & Headroom (Domain 2): Where is the gain originating? Is an overdrive
     pedal's level knob set to 10 with its tone knob cranked, dumping high-frequency
     thermal hiss into a high-gain tube preamp channel that already has its gain at 9?
  2. Source & Performance (Domain 1): Is the guitar unshielded, or picking up CRT/computer
     monitor electromagnetic interference?
  3. Noise Engineering (Domain 6): Is the gate placed at the input, or in the effects
     loop? An input gate can only silence guitar pickup hum; it cannot silence preamp
     tube hiss. An effects loop gate can silence preamp hiss, but can truncate delay tails.
  SYNTHESIS:
  The engineer re-balances gain staging: lowers the overdrive pedal's level from 10
  to 6.5, reduces preamp gain from 8.5 to 6.0 (restoring 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio),
  implements an intelligent dual-stage gate (a sensitive input gate to clamp pickup
  hum before amplification, coupled with a fast loop gate to silence preamp hiss),
  setting the release to 110 ms to preserve natural staccato palm muting.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 8: "Tone is stiff, sterile, and lacks dynamic touch responsiveness."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Add a tube screamer or boost to get more sustain.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Amplifier Behaviour (Domain 8): Is the amplifier solid-state, or a modern high-power
     Class AB fixed-bias design with massive filtering and heavy negative feedback?
     These designs have very high damping and zero power-supply sag, feeling "stiff"
     under the pick.
  2. Dynamics & Envelope (Domain 5): Tube amplifiers with vacuum tube rectifiers
     (e.g., 5U4G, GZ34) experience high internal resistance under heavy current draw,
     dropping plate voltage and creating dynamic "sag" (bloom and soft compression).
  3. Source & Performance (Domain 1): Are the guitar pickups active (EMG style)
     with built-in preamps that compress pick dynamics before the cable?
  SYNTHESIS:
  Adding a boost pedal makes the tone even stiffer and more compressed. The engineer
  replaces the stiff power section emulation with a cathode-biased, low-feedback
  power stage with tube rectifier sag, allowing the amplifier to breathe and compress
  naturally in response to picking strength.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 9: "Spectrum matches the reference on an RTA, but it still sounds wrong."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AMATEUR REACTION:
  - Apply an even finer 30-band match EQ to eliminate the remaining 1 dB differences.
CROSS-DOMAIN PROFESSIONAL REASONING:
  1. Nonlinear Processing (Domain 3): Real-Time Analyzers (RTAs) show time-averaged
     frequency magnitude; they tell you NOTHING about harmonic distortion texture.
     A clean tone boosted 12 dB at 3 kHz looks identical on an RTA to a saturated
     tone generating 3rd harmonics at 3 kHz, but they sound radically different.
  2. Dynamics & Envelope (Domain 5): An RTA averages energy over time; it cannot
     distinguish between a sharp 10 ms transient and a flat, squashed sustain brick.
  3. Loudspeaker & Cabinet (Domain 9): Mechanical cone breakup produces chaotic,
     dynamic harmonic distortion that cannot be captured by a static linear EQ curve.
  SYNTHESIS:
  The engineer abandons static RTA curve matching. Analyzes the dynamic crest factor
  (revealing the reference has 4 dB more transient attack) and total harmonic
  distortion (revealing the reference is significantly more saturated in the low-mids).
  Redesigns the gain architecture and dynamic envelope to match the physical
  mechanisms, achieving a true acoustic match that RTA curves were blind to.

SECTION 7 — EVIDENCE & UNCERTAINTY STANDARD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7.1 THE SIX EVIDENCE DISCIPLINES
A professional AI Sound Engineer must rigorously categorize every piece of
information it processes:

  1. MEASURED EVIDENCE:
     - Quantifiable, objective data extracted directly from audio signals.
     - Examples: RMS energy, peak-to-RMS crest factor, integrated LUFS, spectral
       tilt (dB/octave), harmonic distortion ratio (THD), noise floor in dBFS.
     - Epistemic Status: High objective confidence. Must be reported with units.

  2. OBSERVED EVIDENCE:
     - Phenomenological characteristics clearly audible upon critical listening,
     - Examples: Symmetrical vs. asymmetrical clipping texture, audible slapback
       echo, prominent cabinet room ambience, fret rattle.
     - Epistemic Status: High perceptual confidence, but qualitative.

  3. INFERRED EVIDENCE:
     - Deductions derived from measured/observed evidence using acoustic first
       principles.
     - Examples: Inferring a closed-back cabinet from steep sub-100 Hz low-end
       rolloff; inferring an off-axis mic from rolled-off 6 kHz highs with preserved
       transients.
     - Epistemic Status: Moderate confidence. Must explicitly document the causal
       chain connecting the observation to the inference.

  4. ASSUMED EVIDENCE:
     - Working hypotheses adopted when direct evidence is unavailable.
     - Examples: Assuming standard guitar tuning (E standard) when no sub-bass
       cues exist; assuming a bridge pickup was used for a thrash rhythm track.
     - Epistemic Status: Low confidence. Must be flagged as assumptions, and the
       system must be prepared to discard them if contradictions arise.

  5. SUPPLIED CONTEXT:
     - Background metadata provided by the user, artist descriptions, or song titles.
     - Examples: "1983 Metallica recording", "I want an AC/DC crunch", "drop D tuning".
     - Epistemic Status: Contextual hint ONLY. Never treated as physical acoustic
       proof. Must be verified against the audio evidence.

  6. UNKNOWN INFORMATION:
     - Critical parameters that cannot be measured, observed, or reliably inferred
       from available data.
     - Examples: Guitar pickup inductance when listening to a heavily mastered mix;
       precise amplifier circuit modifications.
     - Epistemic Status: Zero confidence. Must be explicitly declared as UNKNOWN.

7.2 FALSE PRECISION & CONFIDENCE CALIBRATION
  - Prohibition of False Precision: The Sound Engineer must never emit hyper-specific
    numeric values that cannot be justified by evidence (e.g., specifying mic
    distance as "2.37 cm" or confidence as "94.8%").
  - Calibrated Confidence Scale:
      * 90-100%: Direct, high-resolution isolated stem with unambiguous physical markers.
      * 70-89%: Clear audio reference, minor mix masking, solid inference chain.
      * 50-69%: Complex multi-instrument mix, moderate ambiguity, multiple candidate causes.
      * Below 50%: Heavy masking, poor audio quality, high uncertainty.

7.3 THE "INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE" DECLARATION
A professional engineer must know when to say:
  "INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO DETERMINE A UNIQUE SOLUTION."

Mandatory triggers for declaring insufficient evidence:
  - Audio reference has an SNR below 12 dB or severe MP3 compression artifacts
    masking high frequencies.
  - The guitar signal is buried in a dense mix where drum transients and bass energy
    mask 80% of the guitar's dynamic envelope.
  - Multiple contradictory causal hypotheses have equal acoustic plausibility.
  - The user prompt contains contradictory requests (e.g., "Glassy clean Fender
    tone with heavy scooped Dual Rectifier distortion").

Action upon declaring insufficient evidence:
  1. State clearly what is known and what cannot be determined.
  2. Formulate the primary competing hypotheses.
  3. Propose a robust, versatile baseline solution.
  4. Formulate specific diagnostic questions or tests for the user to resolve
     the ambiguity.

SECTION 8 — PROFESSIONAL DECISION STANDARD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every architectural choice made by the Sound Engineer must be recorded as a formal,
defensible Engineering Decision. A valid Engineering Decision Record must contain
the following eleven elements:

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANATOMY OF A DEFENSIBLE ENGINEERING DECISION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. OBJECTIVE:
   - What specific acoustic or musical goal does this decision aim to achieve?
   - Example: "Tighten low-end palm-mute articulation and eliminate 120 Hz flub."

2. RELEVANT EVIDENCE:
   - What specific measured, observed, or contextual evidence prompted this action?
   - Example: "Measured crest factor drops to 4 dB during palm mutes; observed
     intermodulation hash below 150 Hz; audio reveals heavy low-frequency drive."

3. CAUSAL HYPOTHESIS:
   - What underlying physical mechanism is responsible for the problem/need?
   - Example: "Excessive sub-150 Hz signal voltage from the neck pickup is driving
     the first triode gain stage into blocking distortion."

4. PROPOSED INTERVENTION:
   - What exact processing or structural change is being made?
   - Example: "Insert a pre-gain 18 dB/octave high-pass filter tuned to 115 Hz."

5. CAUSAL RATIONALE:
   - Why will this intervention solve the problem based on audio first principles?
   - Example: "Attenuating low-frequency energy before the non-linear clipping stage
     prevents grid blocking and reduces 2nd-order intermodulation distortion,
     preserving transient snap."

6. EXPECTED EFFECT:
   - What will the resulting sound be?
   - Example: "Palm mutes will exhibit immediate percussive punch, with note
     separation restored during fast riffing."

7. PHYSICAL & LOGICAL CONSTRAINTS:
   - What bounds must this intervention respect?
   - Example: "Filter must not exceed 130 Hz, or the fundamental frequency of
     the low D string (73.4 Hz harmonic) will lose its perceived body."

8. TRADE-OFFS & COSTS:
   - What is being sacrificed or risked?
   - Example: "Acoustic low-end weight is slightly reduced on open single notes;
     must be compensated post-distortion in the cabinet or rack."

9. UPSTREAM / DOWNSTREAM DEPENDENCIES:
   - What other stages are affected by this change?
   - Example: "Downstream amplifier preamp gain can now be increased by 1 dB
     without causing muddiness."

10. CONFIDENCE & UNCERTAINTY:
    - How certain is this decision based on evidence?
    - Example: "Confidence: 85%. Uncertainty: Player's pick gauge is unknown."

11. VERIFICATION CRITERIA:
    - How will we objectively prove this intervention succeeded?
    - Example: "Crest factor during palm mutes should increase from 4 dB to at
      least 7 dB; RTA should show clean harmonic decay between 100-200 Hz."

SECTION 9 — PROFESSIONAL FAILURE MODES / ANTI-PATTERNS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A professional AI Sound Engineer must be explicitly guarded against the following
systemic failure modes:

1. THE RECIPE TRAP (CARGO CULTING):
   - Treating genre labels or artist names as fixed gear presets.
   - Example: Automatically inserting a Tube Screamer and JCM800 whenever the user
     mentions "80s rock," regardless of the user's specific audio or guitar.

2. SYMPTOM SUPPRESSION:
   - Treating acoustic defects where they are heard rather than where they originate.
   - Example: Notching out 4 kHz fizz with a post-cab digital EQ instead of fixing
     the harsh diode clipping or on-axis mic placement that generated it.

3. LOUDNESS DECEPTION:
   - Evaluating setting changes without strict RMS/LUFS level matching, mistaking
     increased volume for superior tone quality.

4. SPECTRAL MYOPIA:
   - Assuming that if two tones have identical static frequency response curves
     on a spectrum analyzer, they are identical, completely ignoring dynamic
     compression, transient attack, and harmonic distortion textures.

5. PROCESSOR BLOAT:
   - Inserting redundant, cascading processors to solve problems that could be
     solved by adjusting existing amplifier or microphone controls.

6. FALSE CERTAINTY & HALLUCINATED PRECISION:
   - Emitting 99% confidence scores or specifying mic distances to the millimeter
     based on low-resolution, heavily masked audio evidence.

7. THE "MORE IS BETTER" TRAP:
   - When an intervention fails or only partially succeeds, blindly increasing the
     setting (e.g., boosting more treble, adding more gain) instead of questioning
     the underlying hypothesis.

8. MONO COLLAPSE IGNORANCE:
   - Designing wide, impressive stereo effects using out-of-phase delays or stereo
     widening without checking that the guitar does not completely disappear when
     summed to mono.

9. UNCHECKED ASSUMPTIONS:
   - Treating contextual hints or inferences as verified facts and refusing to
     revise them when contradictory evidence emerges.

10. PLATFORM ENTANGLEMENT:
    - Allowing the constraints, quirks, or naming conventions of a specific guitar
      modeling software (like AmpliTube 5) to dictate general sound engineering logic.
